Teaching

Two convictions shape how I teach. The first is that students believe economics when they handle the evidence themselves—a dataset, a historical episode, a live policy debate—rather than only watching a model derived at the board. The second is that economic history keeps theory honest: setting a model next to what actually happened in a real economy shows students both where it bites and where it breaks.

In practice, that means my courses ask students to read the empirical work that drives a field, not just the textbook summary of it, and then make an argument of their own. In Economics of Developing Areas, students read papers on disease, colonial institutions, and the slave trades alongside short primers on regression and causality, so no statistics background is needed to follow the evidence. Each student then takes one country’s development record, writes up its history, and presents and defends a policy response to the class.

Courses Taught

I was the instructor of record for four undergraduate courses at George Mason University from 2021 to 2024, in person and online, designing the syllabus, assignments, and exams for each.

ECON 104: Principles of Macroeconomics

Summer 2024 · Online

National income, money and banking, growth, unemployment, and inflation, taught through Cowen and Tabarrok’s Modern Principles, with weekly problem sets and quizzes.

ECON 306: Intermediate Microeconomics

Fall 2023 · Online

Demand, costs, and price and output under different market structures, closing with Hayek’s “The Use of Knowledge in Society” as a capstone on what prices do.

ECON 360: Economics of Developing Areas

Spring 2023, Summer 2022 · In person

Why are some countries rich and others poor? The course moves from Malthus and Solow through poverty traps, the aid debate, health and human capital, institutions, culture, state capacity, and conflict, pairing Easterly, Banerjee and Duflo, and Koyama and Rubin with research by North, Nunn, Dell, and others. Students write discussion essays and present a country case.

ECON 385: International Economic Policy

Spring 2022, Fall 2021 · In person

The economic way of thinking applied to trade and international finance for non-majors, including introductory microeconomics and game theory, with historical and current evidence on the consequences of trade, protectionism, and conflict.

Courses I Would Like to Teach

I am prepared to teach principles and intermediate micro- and macroeconomics, undergraduate econometrics, economic development, and economic history, with notice. I would also like to build two courses of my own:

Institutions and Historical Political Economy

NoneDescription

Why do some societies build institutions that sustain growth and order, while others stall or fall into conflict? This upper-level course pairs the theory of states, violence, and institutional change with the empirical economic history that tests it: guilds and city councils, representative assemblies, colonial institutions, and the long shadow they cast on present-day outcomes. Its centerpiece is a replication exercise: students reproduce a published historical result from the original data, then probe whether it survives a change of their own design.

Building Evidence from the Historical Record

NoneDescription

Most claims about the past were made before anyone could test them with data. This methods seminar teaches students to build the evidence themselves: digitizing and coding primary sources, constructing geo-referenced panels, and finding credible variation in observational data. Working from a shared dataset drawn from my own research—digitized nineteenth-century newspapers or geocoded episodes of medieval unrest—students move through the full pipeline from raw source to defensible result, and leave with a finished piece of original research. No prior programming experience is assumed.
